\subsection{Structure of an Autonomous Robot}
Navilott drives a street-style course without intervention: it follows a lane,
stops at stop signs and stop lines, obeys traffic lights and avoids obstacles.
Its software is divided into six functional areas, the usual chain from sensing
to motion~\citep{douglas2020autonav1}, and each boundary exists because the
task needs it (Figure~\ref{fig:robot-loop}). The areas are defined by what each
hands to the next:

\begin{figure}[H]
      \centering
      \input{figures/tikz/robot_loop}
      \caption{The six areas as a closed loop.}
      \label{fig:robot-loop}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Capture} produces raw measurements: camera frames, gyro and
            accelerometer readings, and wheel encoder counts, each stamped with
            the time it was taken.
      \item \textbf{Perception} turns one frame into what that frame shows: lane
            position, a stop line, a stop sign, a traffic light's color, an
            obstacle.
      \item \textbf{Estimation} turns perception's results and the motion
            sensors into what the robot believes over time: its heading, the
            distance it has traveled, and whether a stop line is really ahead
            rather than seen once.
      \item \textbf{Navigation} turns that belief into a decision: keep the
            lane, stop, wait, or cross an intersection, and at what speed.
      \item \textbf{Control} turns the decision into a duty for each wheel,
            and during turns corrects it against the speed the encoders
            report.
      \item \textbf{Actuation} turns duties into motion: PWM signals switch the
            motor driver, which applies battery voltage to the motors.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Perception and estimation.}
The most consequential boundary is between these two areas. A single frame can
be wrong: glare hides a lane line, another lane's stop line enters the view, a
frame arrives late. Perception therefore reports only what each frame shows,
and estimation decides what to believe by combining frames with each other and
with the sensors the camera cannot replace. The camera measures where the robot
is relative to the lane; the gyro and encoders measure how it is moving.

\paragraph{Two feedback paths.}
What actuation does is measured and returned, so each decision is based on what
the robot actually did. The loop closes along two paths:

\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Within control}: the encoders report each wheel's
            speed every frame. During lane keeping, control learns from it how
            much speed each unit of duty produces; during a turn, it corrects
            each wheel's duty against the commanded speed, so the turn keeps
            its shape whatever the battery voltage or the floor.
      \item \textbf{Across frames}, through the camera and estimation: the
            next frame shows where the last command put the robot in the lane,
            and the gyro and encoders show how far it turned and traveled, so
            the next decision corrects any error the last one left.
\end{itemize}

Keeping this loop running at the camera's frame rate is the constraint behind
every later decision.

\subsection{Single-Board Design}\label{sec:single_board}
The first decision is where each functional area runs, and in particular how
perception on the camera side connects to actuation at the motors. Four
criteria decided it: the control rate the robot actually needs, a bill of
materials within the \$100 budget, processing done on the robot rather than
off-board, and the development time a second processor would add.

Three options were considered (Figure~\ref{fig:board-options}):

\begin{figure}[H]
      \centering
      \input{figures/tikz/board_options}
      \caption{Where each area runs under the three options.}
      \label{fig:board-options}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{MCU-only}: an STM32 or Arduino runs perception and drives
            the motors.
      \item \textbf{Pi + MCU}: the Pi runs perception and sends a packet over
            UART to an STM32 or Arduino, which runs a fast PID loop and
            generates the PWM.
      \item \textbf{Pi-only}: the Pi runs perception and drives the motor
            driver directly with PWM.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{MCU-only: feasible, but against the criteria.}
Microcontrollers can run camera-based vision: parts such as the STM32H7 and
ESP32-S3 accept a parallel digital video port (DVP) camera and have enough
memory, internal or external, for small frames. The option was rejected for
what it would cost this design:

\textbf{First, the camera}: the robot needs a wide field of view to see the lane lines
and the traffic light from a stop line (Section~\ref{sec:capture}), and the
wide-angle sensors considered (IMX219, IMX290) are raw Bayer sensors on MIPI
CSI-2 with no on-sensor image processing. They need a host with a CSI-2
receiver and an ISP, which most microcontrollers in the budget lack; a DVP
camera would avoid that but would also take roughly a third of the I/O the
motors, encoders and sensors need.

\textbf{Second, the software}: the pipeline undistorts a wide-angle lens, converts color
spaces, finds contours and maps the image to the ground, all through OpenCV,
and every phase is tested off the robot on recorded frames. On a
microcontroller each of those would be reimplemented in C, without the replay
and recording tools the design relies on.

A single $480\times270$ color frame is also 389\,KB of raw pixel storage before
any intermediate images, which fits only the largest microcontrollers or
external PSRAM, at a cost in memory bandwidth.

\paragraph{Pi + MCU: more flexible, but with more overhead.}
In this layout the Pi runs perception and sends a command packet over UART to a
microcontroller, which runs the wheel control loop, generates the PWM and reads
the encoders (Appendix~\ref{app:repository} gives the full specification and
packet format). It has three real benefits:

\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Motor loop}: the motor loop runs at its own rate, independent of the frame rate and of Linux
            scheduling.
      \item \textbf{Encoders}: the encoders are decoded by the microcontroller's timer hardware rather than by
            software.
      \item \textbf{Motor safety}: the motor safety no longer depends on the Pi: a watchdog on the microcontroller
            brakes the motors if commands stop for any reason, including the Pi's process
            dying or the Pi losing power.
\end{itemize}

Its costs are a second processor's worth of design: a packet protocol with
framing, checksums and a defined response to a lost link; two clocks to
reconcile, since frames are stamped on the Pi and encoder and IMU samples on
the microcontroller, where an error misaligns the two without any visible
failure; and two codebases to build, test and keep in step.

\paragraph{Decision: Pi-only.}
The Pi Zero 2 W runs every area. Choosing it means giving up the three benefits
of the microcontroller, and the design was judged against each.

\textbf{First}, a faster motor loop, buys little here: steering corrections come
from the lane offset, which changes once per frame, so the camera sets the
control rate in either layout, and a loop far faster than 20\,Hz would mostly
repeat the last command.

\textbf{Second}, encoders decoded in hardware, is largely
provided by the pigpio daemon, which samples the pins in native code and queues
every edge; a late Python thread delays the counts but does not lose them. The
same daemon drives the Pi's two hardware PWM channels (GPIO12 and GPIO13), so
the waveform holds steady when the software is late, and scheduling delay
postpones a change of command without distorting the signal.

\textbf{Third}, motor supervision independent of the Pi's software, is given up,
and the conditions under which that is acceptable are set out below.

In return, the robot has one board, one clock and one codebase, which every
phase can be tested against off the robot. The cost is that timing and safety
now depend on one Linux system: control shares the processor with perception,
and stopping the motors depends on the Pi's software.
Sections~\ref{sec:background_threads} and~\ref{sec:linux_scheduler} describe
how the design limits those costs. That 20\,Hz is sufficient is an assumption
of the design, not a result of it; Section~\ref{sec:results} tests it on the
course.

\paragraph{The residual hazard and why it is accepted.}
Without a second processor, every software path that can stop the motors runs
inside the robot's Python process. If that process is killed, or hangs while
holding the interpreter lock, neither the watchdog nor the run loop can act,
and the pigpio daemon continues to generate PWM at the last commanded duty: the
robot keeps driving on its last command. A reset or loss of power on the Pi
does not carry this hazard, because the motor driver's standby pin returns to
its default low state and disables the outputs; the hazard is specific to a
failed process on a running Pi.

The design accepts this under one criterion: a runaway robot must be unable to
harm anyone and must always be stoppable by hand. The robot meets it on the
course because it is small and slow (1Kg, top speed about 4\,cm/s at the base
duty), its N20 motors stall against light resistance, the course is enclosed
and kept clear of people, and an operator is present for every run, since no
run can start without the start button. The physical intervention is to lift
the robot, which takes the wheels off the floor, and to open the power switch,
which removes the motors' supply.

A hardware-independent disable was considered: a retriggerable timer on the
driver's standby pin, held enabled only while the software toggles a GPIO every
frame, would cut the motors whenever the process stopped, whatever its state.
It was not adopted because the criterion above was already met for the course,
and it is the first change recommended for any use outside the course
(Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\paragraph{Sensing.}
The camera was the one component replaced during development, and the change
moved work from geometry to software. The original Raspberry Pi Camera V2
(IMX219) cropped its field of view in the sensor mode the Pi selected, leaving
a 22\,cm blind spot ahead of the robot: stopped at a stop line, it could not
see the lane lines and the traffic light in the same frame, so the stopping
position traded one against the other. The IMX290-based replacement uses the
same CSI-2 interface and reduces the blind spot to 10\,cm, so a single stopping
position serves both. Its wider lens adds barrel distortion, which perception
removes on every frame using a per-lens calibration; this adds a fixed
processing step and ties the calibration to the lens and its mounting
(Section~\ref{sec:capture}). The camera alone measures where the robot is
relative to the lane, while the IMU and wheel encoders supply heading and wheel
motion to estimation (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}).

\paragraph{Motion.}
The Pi's GPIO pins supply only a few milliamps, far below what the N20 motors
draw, so a TB6612FNG H-bridge switches battery power to them under PWM from the
Pi. The driver can also short-brake a motor (connect its terminals); the
software uses this for every stop.

\paragraph{Power.}
One 3S pack powers everything along two paths: directly to the motor driver,
and through a buck converter to the Pi's 5\,V supply, from which the Pi
supplies 3.3\,V to the logic-level parts. Motor current lowers the pack voltage
under load, and the buck converter keeps the Pi's supply steady as long as the
pack stays above its minimum input. The Pi has no analog input, so an ADS1115
reads the pack voltage through a 4:1 divider.

\paragraph{Battery protection and its limits.}
The software's voltage thresholds protect the pack against over-discharge
during a run: it warns at 10.5\,V and ends the run after three consecutive
readings at or below 9.9\,V, an average of 3.3\,V per cell. The reading is
smoothed, so a brief sag under motor load does not end a run, but a sustained
heavy load reads lower than the resting voltage and can end it early. The
thresholds cannot guarantee more than that. They see only the pack's total, so
one weak cell can fall below a safe voltage while the average stays above the
limit; they act only while the robot's software runs, while the Pi and buck
converter continue to draw from the pack when it does not; and they offer no
protection against over-current or a short circuit. The cells carry their own
protection circuits, which covers those cases. Charging is done off the robot
with a balance charger, which also restores equal cell voltages between runs.

\paragraph{Interface.}
A start button holds the robot until the operator is ready: no motor command is
sent before it is pressed. A TM1637 display shows the robot's state and the run
time, and the final time when the run ends.

\paragraph{Integration.}
Every component except the camera connects through a PCB that carries the power
rails and signal routing, with soldered headers for each external part. The
chassis holds what the board cannot: the motors, the camera at a fixed height
and angle, the display, the button and the battery. Fixing the camera mount
matters beyond packaging: the lens calibration and the stop-line distance table
are valid only for one mounting position, so moving the camera means repeating
both.

\subsection{Software Language}
With the hardware interfaces established, the next decision is the language
that turns sensor data into motor commands. Four criteria decided it: access to
a mature computer-vision library, a path from the Pi's camera stack into that
library, fast iteration on the robot, and code that can be tested off the
robot. Python met all four:

\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{OpenCV}: the computer-vision library, written in C++, with
            Python bindings for every algorithm the pipeline uses.
      \item \textbf{GStreamer}: OpenCV opens the camera through a GStreamer
            pipeline built on libcamera, so frames arrive from the Pi's camera stack
            directly as OpenCV images.
      \item \textbf{Iteration}: no compile step; a change is edited on the Pi
            and run immediately, which suits tuning thresholds on the course.
      \item \textbf{Testing}: replacing the camera, sensors and motors with test
            doubles is direct in Python, so every phase is tested on a laptop with
            synthetic or recorded frames.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The cost: an interpreter and a global lock.}
Python is interpreted, so each Python-level operation costs far more than its
equivalent in C. It also has a Global Interpreter Lock (GIL): only one thread
executes Python code at a time, whichever core it is on. Native code called
from Python may release the lock while it works, but only where the library
does so explicitly, so the cost of the GIL depends on which calls a thread
spends its time in:

\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{OpenCV}: the generated Python bindings release the lock for
            the duration of each wrapped C++ function, including
            \texttt{VideoCapture.read()}, which waits for the next frame without
            it. Converting NumPy arguments to and from OpenCV images happens
            with the lock held, but contiguous arrays are not copied.
      \item \textbf{GStreamer and libcamera}: run on their own native threads
            and never take the lock.
      \item \textbf{NumPy}: releases the lock for many whole-array operations
            on numeric data, but not for indexing, small arrays, or work that
            loops in Python.
      \item \textbf{Python code}: everything between those calls (iterating
            over contours, applying gates, building packets, evaluating
            navigation rules) holds the lock throughout.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why the cost is acceptable.}
The pipeline keeps per-pixel work inside OpenCV calls and leaves Python to
connect them and handle their results. Rewriting estimation and navigation in
C++ would save under 1\% of the frame (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The
perception stage times include the Python that drives their OpenCV calls, such
as geometry's loop over candidate contours, and that share was not measured
separately; a C++ port could shorten perception by that share, but no more,
since the OpenCV work is already native. The conclusion is limited to this
workload: a pipeline with more Python per pixel would weigh the choice
differently. The GIL does constrain the threads that run alongside the main
loop, which the next section describes.

\subsection{Background Threads}\label{sec:background_threads}
Python and its native libraries define the implementation environment, but not
how sensing, processing and motor supervision run at the same time. Navilott
runs as one Python process plus the pigpio daemon
(Figure~\ref{fig:execution-model}). The main loop does the per-frame work;
every other path either collects data at its own rate or supervises the main
loop, and each device has exactly one owner, so no two paths contend for the
same hardware. Every frame and reading is stamped on the same monotonic clock,
so data from different paths line up without a synchronization step.
Table~\ref{tab:execution-paths} gives each path's rate and what it does when
the main loop falls behind.

\paragraph{Three freshness policies.}
The paths that feed the main loop keep their data in three different ways,
because each kind of data loses its value differently with age:

\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Frames are newest-only.} A frame describes the scene at one
            instant, and an old one is worse than none, so the appsink keeps a
            single frame. A late loop processes the current scene and never
            works through a backlog; the frames it missed are simply lost.
      \item \textbf{IMU readings are a bounded history.} Heading is the
            integral of yaw rate, so every reading between frames contributes,
            and the hub keeps them all for the next frame. The buffer holds
            2\,s; if the loop stalls longer, the oldest readings are dropped
            and the frame reports how many. Estimation further clamps the time
            step it integrates over to 0.5\,s, so after a long stall the
            heading under-counts any turn made during it; lane vision, or the
            gyro target of a turn in progress, must then recover the error.
      \item \textbf{Encoder counts are stateful.} The callbacks add
            each edge to a running total, and each frame reads the total
            afresh, so distance is exact however late the read. Only the speed
            derived from it degrades, since it becomes an average over a longer
            window.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The GIL and the sensor hub.}
Of the background paths, only the sensor hub runs Python on a deadline: one
reading every 10\,ms. When the main loop is inside an OpenCV call, which
releases the lock, the hub runs immediately; when the main loop is running
Python, the hub waits for the interpreter to switch threads. Python requests a
switch after 5\,ms, but the hand-over happens only when the running thread
reaches a point where it can release the lock, so a reading can be delayed by a
sizeable fraction of its period. Two measures limit the effect. The hub holds
the lock as briefly as possible: the IMU's 14-byte sample block is read in one
I\textsuperscript{2}C transfer at 400\,kHz, so most of each reading is spent in
the bus driver. And each reading is stamped when it is taken, so a delay
changes when the reading is processed but not the time it is integrated at.
Python's garbage collector, which can pause every thread at an unpredictable
moment, is disabled during a run and collects only when the robot is braked or
every 200 frames.

\subsection{Linux Scheduler}\label{sec:linux_scheduler}
The execution model above depends on each thread getting a core when it needs
one, and on Linux that is the scheduler's decision. The engineering contract
follows from what the scheduler does and does not promise. The Pi runs kernel
6.18.50 (\texttt{rpt-rpi-v8}) with a preemptible configuration
(\texttt{PREEMPT}), whose default policy is Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline
First (EEVDF). It shares CPU time among runnable tasks and preempts them at
will, but it is not a hard real-time scheduler: it gives ordinary threads no
bound on when they will run. Navilott does not take control of the scheduler
(no core pinning, no real-time priorities). The design therefore treats every
path as one that can be late, tolerates lateness wherever it can, and limits
what lateness can cause where it cannot (Table~\ref{tab:timing-failures}).

\paragraph{How much lateness each path tolerates.}
The contract can be stated per path, as the delay each one absorbs without any
effect on the robot's behaviour:

\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Sensor readings}: a late reading is still integrated at its
            true time, so delays are absorbed up to the 2\,s buffer, and heading
            stays exact across frame gaps of up to 0.5\,s.
      \item \textbf{Frames}: a late frame delays one command but creates no
            backlog, so lateness never accumulates from one frame to the next.
      \item \textbf{Commands}: the motors hold the previous duty, and the
            watchdog intervenes only after 0.5\,s, ten frame periods, without a
            new command.
\end{itemize}

These are design bounds, not measured ones. The design additionally expects
ordinary scheduling delay to be far shorter than any of them, since the Pi has
four cores and fewer busy threads than cores for most of each frame. That is an
expectation, not a property of the scheduler: it holds only as far as it has
been measured, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the measured wake-up
latency distribution and the worst delay observed. Real-time priorities would
narrow the delay, at the risk of a runaway thread starving the rest of the
system, and are left as an option if the measured delays approach the bounds
above.

\paragraph{Three different stops, one unresolved.}
The table separates three conditions that are easy to treat as one. When the
\emph{command stream} stops but the process runs, the watchdog brakes the
motors. When the \emph{application} stops through an exception, the run loop
brakes them on its way out. When the \emph{process} itself stops, or the
interpreter hangs, no software on the Pi stops the motors: pigpiod outlives the
process and keeps the last PWM duty. The first two are handled in software. The
third is an unresolved safety limitation of the single-board design, accepted
under the conditions in Section~\ref{sec:single_board} and covered only by
stopping the robot by hand.

With the runtime's timing limits and their mitigations established, the
remaining concern is defining the software architecture.
